EPCG Scheme Documentation Mistakes Importers Should Avoid

The Export Promotion Capital Goods (EPCG) Scheme is an important facility for eligible Indian exporters and manufacturers who need to import capital goods for pre-production, production, and post-production activities. However, using the scheme also involves documentation and compliance responsibilities.

From applying for an EPCG authorisation to importing machinery, obtaining an installation certificate, and fulfilling export obligations, every stage requires accurate records. Missing documents, incorrect machinery descriptions, mismatched import details, and delayed submissions can create avoidable compliance difficulties.

For importers and manufacturers in Chennai, understanding these common EPCG documentation mistakes can help improve record management and prepare for inspections and submissions.

What Is the EPCG Scheme?

The Export Promotion Capital Goods Scheme is administered by the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT), under India's Foreign Trade Policy. It facilitates eligible exporters in importing capital goods under specified customs duty benefits, subject to the scheme's conditions and export-obligation requirements.

Capital goods eligible under the scheme typically include:

  • Manufacturing and production machinery
  • Industrial processing equipment
  • CNC machines and machine tools
  • Testing and quality-control equipment
  • Computer systems forming part of capital goods
  • Eligible spares, moulds, dies, jigs, fixtures, and tools

The eligibility of specific goods and the applicable conditions must be checked against the current Foreign Trade Policy and the EPCG authorisation.

10 EPCG Scheme Documentation Mistakes Importers Should Avoid

01. Submitting an Incomplete EPCG Authorisation Application

One common mistake is submitting an EPCG application without checking whether all required information and supporting documents are available. Incorrect applicant details, incomplete machinery descriptions, missing technical information, or insufficient supporting documents can lead to queries or deficiencies from the Regional Authority.

How to avoid it:
  • Verify IEC and applicant details.
  • Check the required application form and supporting documents.
  • Review machinery descriptions and technical specifications.
  • Ensure the required certificates are included.
  • Check that the application is properly authorised and digitally signed.

02. Incorrect Machinery Description and Technical Specifications

The machinery description in the EPCG authorisation should be reviewed carefully against the proposed import details. Differences in the machine name, model, capacity, technical specifications, or quantity may create difficulties during import documentation and subsequent verification.

How to avoid it:
  • Maintain a detailed machinery list.
  • Verify manufacturer and model information.
  • Check capacity and technical specifications.
  • Compare the proposed import details with the authorisation.
  • Obtain the necessary amendment before proceeding where required.

03. Missing or Incorrect Nexus Certificate

A Nexus Certificate from an independent Chartered Engineer is required in the relevant EPCG authorisation process under the applicable procedure. The certificate establishes the technical relationship between the capital goods proposed for import and the export product or service. Errors in machinery descriptions, production details, technical capacity, or the stated nexus may create issues during application review.

How to avoid it:
  • Provide accurate production and machinery information.
  • Clearly describe the proposed capital goods.
  • Explain the relationship between machinery and export production.
  • Verify all technical details before certification.
  • Ensure the certificate is prepared by a qualified independent Chartered Engineer acting within their area of competence.

04. Mismatch Between the EPCG Authorisation and Bill of Entry

The Bill of Entry contains important information about imported machinery, including descriptions, quantities, values, and other import particulars. If these details do not correspond with the EPCG authorisation, the importer may face difficulties during customs verification.

How to avoid it:
  • Cross-check the authorisation against the Bill of Entry.
  • Verify the machinery description and quantity.
  • Check the port of registration.
  • Review the import particulars before filing.
  • Maintain copies of the final customs-cleared documents.

05. Not Maintaining Complete Machinery Installation Records

Importers should maintain evidence showing where and how the imported capital goods have been installed. Missing installation photographs, incomplete factory information, or incorrect machinery identification details can make certification and verification more difficult.

How to avoid it:
  • Record the installation location.
  • Maintain machinery photographs.
  • Keep serial number and model details.
  • Preserve installation and commissioning records.
  • Maintain relevant factory and supporting manufacturer information.

06. Delaying the EPCG Installation Certificate

The installation certificate is an important EPCG compliance document. Under the amendment introduced through DGFT Public Notice No. 15/2024-25 dated 25 July 2024, the general time limit for submitting the installation certificate for capital goods was extended to three years from the date of completion of import. The Regional Authority may allow an extension up to the valid export-obligation period, subject to a composition fee of ₹10,000 per year. Where an independent Chartered Engineer's certificate is used, a copy must also be sent to the jurisdictional Customs authority for intimation or record.

How to avoid it:
  • Record the import completion date.
  • Track the applicable submission deadline.
  • Arrange machinery inspection in advance.
  • Prepare supporting documents before certification.
  • Check current DGFT provisions before relying on an extension.

07. Not Updating Records When Machinery Is Shifted

Businesses may shift capital goods between eligible units during the export-obligation period. However, shifting machinery without checking the applicable EPCG conditions and completing the required documentation can create compliance problems. The relevant procedure requires a fresh installation certificate to be produced to the Regional Authority within six months of shifting in the circumstances covered by the provision.

How to avoid it:
  • Check whether the proposed destination unit is eligible.
  • Review the IEC and RCMC details.
  • Maintain records of the machinery transfer.
  • Arrange the required fresh installation certificate.
  • Update the relevant DGFT records.

08. Poor Export Obligation Documentation

EPCG authorisation holders must fulfil the applicable export obligations and maintain supporting evidence. Missing shipping bills, incomplete export records, discrepancies in export product details, or missing realisation information may affect the export-obligation discharge process.

How to avoid it:
  • Maintain shipping bill details.
  • Preserve export invoices and related records.
  • Track specific and annual average export obligations separately.
  • Reconcile export information with the authorisation.
  • Maintain relevant e-BRC and export realisation information wherever applicable.

09. Missing Annual Export Obligation Reports and Deadlines

EPCG authorisation holders must monitor the applicable reporting and export-obligation timelines. For authorisations governed by the current Handbook of Procedures, the annual report on fulfilment of export obligation is generally required to be submitted online to the Regional Authority by 30 June each year. Missing deadlines or submitting incomplete details can lead to avoidable compliance issues.

How to avoid it:
  • Maintain an export-obligation calendar.
  • Track annual reporting dates.
  • Reconcile shipping bills and export values.
  • Review specific and annual average obligation requirements.
  • Monitor the first and second block periods.

10. Not Maintaining Records Until EODC and After Redemption

Completing exports does not automatically mean the EPCG authorisation is closed. The authorisation holder must submit the required documents and apply for the Export Obligation Discharge Certificate (EODC) through the applicable DGFT process. Under paragraph 5.22 of the Handbook of Procedures 2023, EPCG authorisation holders must maintain true and proper accounts of exports, supplies, and services rendered towards export-obligation fulfilment for two years from the date of redemption.

How to avoid it:
  • Keep all import and export records organised.
  • Reconcile export-obligation calculations.
  • Submit the applicable EODC application.
  • Respond to deficiencies raised by the Regional Authority.
  • Preserve records for the prescribed period after redemption.

EPCG Scheme Documentation Mistakes vs. Statutory Impact & Corrective Action

To help importers quickly identify risk areas and take corrective compliance steps, the following reference table summarizes the critical EPCG documentation mistakes and their statutory solutions:

Documentation Area Common Importer Mistake Customs & DGFT Risk Correct Compliance Procedure
Application Filing Submitting an incomplete EPCG Authorisation application with generic machine descriptions or unverified process flows. Application rejection, multiple DGFT deficiency memos, and delayed port import clearances. Provide comprehensive technical specifications, accurate process flowcharts, and verified capital goods classifications before submission.
Technical Specifications Using vague naming conventions that do not match the manufacturer invoice or technical catalogue. Customs examination holds, re-assessment delays, and potential dispute over duty concessions at port. Ensure exact brand, model number, rated power capacity, and serial number are clearly endorsed in the EPCG schedule.
Nexus Certificate Importing capital equipment without obtaining an independent Chartered Engineer Nexus Certificate. Disallowance of concessional zero-duty benefit; duty demand with commercial interest penalties. Engage a registered Chartered Engineer to certify that the imported machinery directly links to export product manufacturing.
Bill of Entry Alignment Discrepancies in invoice numbers, port of registration, or item descriptions between Bill of Entry and EPCG license. Customs clearance deadlock; requires costly and time-consuming amendment at the DGFT Regional Authority (RA). Thoroughly verify pre-shipment invoices, packing lists, and draft Bills of Entry against the EPCG license before customs filing.
Installation Certificate Timeline Failing to conduct physical inspection and obtain the Chartered Engineer Installation Certificate within 6 months. Show Cause Notices (SCN), penalty proceedings, and potential forfeiture of the customs performance bond. Arrange physical plant inspection immediately upon commissioning; file Certificate with DGFT RA and Customs within statutory deadlines.
Inter-Unit Shifting Relocating EPCG machinery between corporate factory units or branches without prior DGFT endorsement. Treated as unauthorized diversion of concessional capital goods under the Foreign Trade (D&R) Act. Submit a formal factory address amendment to DGFT RA and obtain an updated Chartered Engineer installation endorsement before relocation.
Export Obligation Documentation Failing to link shipping bill numbers to the specific EPCG license or mismanaging electronic bank realization certificates (e-BRC). Failure to satisfy Export Obligation (EO) audits; recovery of foregone customs duties plus 15% annual interest. Maintain a dedicated export register linking every shipping bill, commercial invoice, and e-BRC to the specific EPCG license.
EODC & Record Retention Discarding documentation after redemption or lacking original customs-endorsed installation certificates. Serious vulnerability during post-clearance audits (PCA) by Customs or Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI). Safely archive all original EPCG files, CE certificates, Bills of Entry, and DGFT Redemption Letters for at least 5 years post-redemption.

EPCG Scheme Documentation Checklist for Importers

Before applying, importing, or submitting compliance documents, importers can use this checklist to verify their documentation records:

Authorisation Documents

  • IEC profile and RCMC details
  • Application form and declarations
  • Machinery specifications and manufacturer details
  • Nexus Certificate where applicable

Import Documents

  • EPCG Authorisation copy
  • Bill of Entry and commercial invoices
  • Packing list and technical documentation
  • Customs clearance records

Installation Records

  • Factory address verification
  • Machinery serial number & nameplate data
  • Clear installation photographs
  • Chartered Engineer Installation Certificate

Export Obligation Records

  • Shipping bills mentioning EPCG details
  • Export invoices and e-BRC / realization data
  • Annual export reports submitted by 30 June
  • EODC application and DGFT redemption letter

How a Chartered Engineer Supports EPCG Documentation

An independent Chartered Engineer plays an important technical role in the EPCG process by providing objective inspection and certification support within their competence. Their role may include:

  • Nexus Certification: Certifying the technical connection between imported capital goods and export production during the authorisation stage.
  • Machinery Inspection: Verifying equipment on-site at factory premises against import invoices, packing lists, and authorisations.
  • Installation Certification: Issuing the prescribed Installation Certificate under applicable DGFT procedures with machinery identification, location details, and photographs.
  • Unit Shifting Verification: Conducting fresh inspections and issuing fresh installation certificates when capital goods are shifted between eligible manufacturing units.
