Importing second-hand machinery into India requires careful attention to customs valuation, import documentation and applicable regulations. Businesses purchasing used industrial equipment from overseas suppliers should understand how customs value is determined before planning their import costs.

The customs value of used machinery in India generally starts with the transaction value, subject to applicable legal conditions and adjustments. The valuation process may involve examining the purchase price, freight, insurance, machinery specifications and supporting transaction documents.

What Is the Customs Value of Used Machinery?

Customs value is the value determined under Indian customs law for calculating customs duties on imported goods where the duties are value-based (ad valorem).

Under Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962, the transaction value is generally the starting point, subject to the conditions and adjustments prescribed by law. You can refer to the official Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962 on the CBIC website for the statutory basis of customs valuation.

The applicable procedures are also covered by the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007.

The customs value is not necessarily the same as the current resale value, book value or depreciated value of machinery. Each figure serves a different purpose. How Is the Customs Value of Used Machinery Calculated in India?

1. Start With the Transaction Value

The transaction value is generally the price actually paid or payable for the imported machinery when sold for export to India, provided the relevant legal conditions are satisfied.

For example, if an Indian importer purchases a used industrial machine from an overseas supplier for â‚¹8,00,000, this price may form the starting point for customs valuation. The declared price must be supported by appropriate documentation and meet the conditions prescribed by the valuation rules.

2. Include Applicable Valuation Adjustments

Certain costs and payments may need to be added to the transaction value if they are not already included and are required to be included under the applicable rules. These may include:

  • Freight and transportation costs to the place of importation
  • Insurance costs
  • Applicable loading, unloading and handling charges
  • Packing costs
  • Certain commissions and brokerage charges
  • Qualifying royalties and licence fees
  • Other additions prescribed by the valuation rules

The precise treatment depends on the nature of each charge and the applicable legal provisions. For details, refer to the official Customs Valuation Rules, 2007.

3. Consider the Age and Condition of the Machinery

The age, physical condition and technical specifications of used machinery may be relevant to an inspection or valuation assessment.

A professional inspection may help establish the year of manufacture, machinery identity, visible wear, missing components and operational condition, where testing is within the agreed scope. Importers can review our step-by-step documentation guide on Chartered Engineer Certificate for Customs in Chennai or our all-India overview on Chartered Engineer Certificate for Customs in India.

However, customs value is not automatically calculated by deducting a fixed depreciation percentage from the price of a new machine. The applicable valuation method must be determined in accordance with the rules and the circumstances of the transaction.

4. Review the Supporting Documents

Customs authorities may examine the documents supporting the declared value, including the commercial invoice, purchase contract, payment evidence, machinery specifications and available inspection or valuation reports.

If there are reasonable grounds to doubt the truth or accuracy of the declared value, the authorities may examine whether the transaction value can be accepted under the applicable procedure. The Chennai Customs Import Procedure explains the documentation, assessment and examination processes applicable to imported goods.

5. Apply Other Valuation Methods Where Required

If the transaction value cannot be accepted, the applicable customs valuation rules prescribe alternative methods in a defined sequence, subject to the relevant provisions:

  • Transaction value of identical goods
  • Transaction value of similar goods
  • Deductive value method
  • Computed value method
  • Residual or fallback method

The appropriate method depends on the available information and the legal conditions for its application. Refer to the official Customs Valuation Rules, 2007 for the prescribed valuation methods.

What Factors Affect the Customs Value of Used Machinery?

Purchase Price

The actual price paid or payable is generally the starting point when the transaction value method applies. A genuine invoice and supporting payment records can help substantiate the declared price.

Machinery Age

The year of manufacture helps identify the equipment and may be relevant to a technical or alternative valuation assessment.

Physical and Operational Condition

Wear, corrosion, damaged components, refurbishment and operating condition can affect the machinery's market value and may be relevant to an inspection.

Manufacturer and Model

The manufacturer, model, production capacity and technical specifications help establish the identity of the machinery and identify potentially comparable equipment.

Freight and Insurance

Transportation and insurance costs may form part of the customs value as prescribed by the applicable valuation rules.

Relationship Between Buyer and Seller

Where the buyer and seller are related, the transaction value must satisfy the relevant legal conditions. The relationship and its effect on the price may need to be examined. For the legal requirements applicable to these factors, refer to the Customs Valuation Rules, 2007.

Documents Required for Used Machinery Customs Valuation

Importers should prepare the available documentation before arranging an inspection or requesting a valuation assessment. Commonly relevant documents include:

  • Commercial invoice
  • Purchase agreement or sales contract
  • Packing list
  • Bill of Lading or Air Waybill
  • Bill of Entry and relevant import declaration details
  • Manufacturer, model and serial number details
  • Year of manufacture evidence
  • Machinery specifications and technical catalogue
  • Payment records, where relevant
  • Freight and insurance documents
  • Photographs of the machinery
  • Previous inspection or valuation reports, where available

Additional documents may be required depending on the equipment, transaction and applicable customs procedure. For general information about import documentation and clearance, refer to the Chennai Customs Import Procedure. Importers should confirm the precise requirements with their customs broker or the relevant customs authority.
