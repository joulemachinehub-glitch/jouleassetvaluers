Empty Shipping Container Inspection: Checklist and Common Defects

Introduction

Shipping containers play an indispensable role in international trade, multi-modal transportation, import logistics, and industrial supply chains. Every day, thousands of TEUs (Twenty-foot Equivalent Units) carry high-value manufactured goods, bulk commodities, industrial machinery, and perishable cargo across oceanic routes, container freight stations (CFS), inland container depots (ICD), and port terminals.

However, before loading any cargo into a container, verifying its physical condition, weather-tightness, and structural soundness is an essential operational requirement. Damaged timber flooring, pinhole roof leaks, warped corner posts, defective locking gears, door seal failures, and severe corrosion can result in catastrophic cargo damage, transit wetting, contamination, customs rejections, and protracted insurance liability disputes.

An Empty Shipping Container Inspection systematically assesses and documents an unladen container's physical integrity before cargo stuffing commences. It provides decisive evidentiary protection for shipping lines, freight forwarders, cargo owners, exporters, and container leasing operators.

Key Operational Takeaway:

Arranging an independent pre-loading container survey guarantees that only sound, clean, and structurally compliant containers enter international transit, mitigating cargo rejection risks before bills of lading are issued.

What Is Empty Shipping Container Inspection?

Empty shipping container inspection is the technical evaluation of an unladen, cleared intermodal container to verify its physical state, cleanliness, structural geometry, weatherproofing, and overall suitability for its planned cargo assignment.

The survey is conducted in accordance with international cargo container conventions such as the International Convention for Safe Containers (CSC 1972) and guidelines issued by the Institute of International Container Lessors (IICL). A professional inspection typically covers:

Container Identification

Verification of ISO container prefix, 6-digit serial number, check digit, and CSC safety approval plate.

Exterior Structure

Detailed appraisal of corner castings, corner posts, bottom side rails, top side rails, and end frames.

Roof Weather-Tightness

Internal light-leakage testing and roof panel checks for pinholes, punctures, and deep indentations.

Floor & Cross-Members

Examination of marine plywood flooring, delamination, soft spots, chemical staining, and forklift load capacity.

Door & Locking Mechanism

Testing hinges, locking rods, cams, retainers, and elastomeric EPDM rubber door gaskets.

Photographic Reporting

High-resolution photographic capture of all 4 elevations, roof, interior, and close-ups of all identified defects.

Why Is Empty Container Inspection Important?

Surveying an empty container before dispatching it to a loading bay or factory warehouse is one of the most effective risk-mitigation measures in ocean freight logistics:

1. Identifying Structural Damage

Corner posts, bottom rails, and cross-members withstand massive stacking loads aboard container vessels (often stacked 9–10 tiers high). Structural cracks, bends, or post distortions must be detected before severe compressive forces cause hull collapse.

2. Preventing Water Leakage & Condensation

Corrosion holes, roof cracks, and deteriorated door gaskets allow sea spray and tropical monsoon rainwater to flood container interiors. Early detection preserves moisture-sensitive commodities like food grains, pharmaceuticals, textiles, and electronics.

3. Protecting Cargo from Chemical & Biological Contamination

Containers previously carrying chemicals, toxic residues, fertilisers, or rotting agricultural produce may retain persistent odours, pest infestations, or hazardous residues that contaminate subsequent shipments.

4. Checking Door Seal Security & Customs Compliance

Misaligned door locking rods, broken cams, or damaged bolt-seal holes compromise seal integrity, increasing theft vulnerabilities and triggering customs scrutiny during border gate verification.

5. Maintaining Legal & Insurance Evidentiary Records

A timestamped survey report with photographic proof establishes baseline container condition, shielding freight forwarders and shippers from unjustified demurrage penalties or damage claims filed by shipping lines.

6. Smooth Port Terminal & Gateway Dispatch

Major maritime container terminals across Chennai, Kamarajar, and JNPT reject damaged containers at in-gates, causing costly vehicle detentions, missed shipping cut-offs, and cancelled export bookings.

8-Point Empty Container Inspection Checklist

Our certified marine surveyors and Chartered Engineers employ an 8-point physical inspection protocol covering all primary structural and functional assemblies:

01

Container Identification & CSC Safety Plate

Verify that the container markings conform to ISO 6346 / BIC international standards:

  • Owner Code & Serial: 4-letter owner code (e.g. MSKU, MEDU), 6-digit serial number, and check digit match all side, roof, and door markings.
  • ISO Size & Type Code: Correct dimensional markings (e.g. 22G1 for 20ft dry, 45G1 for 40ft High Cube).
  • CSC Approval Plate: Clean, legible stainless steel plate indicating country of approval, date of manufacture, Maximum Gross Weight (MGW), allowable stacking weight (typically 192,000 kg), and transverse racking test load values.
  • Payload Markings: Verification that Max Gross Weight minus Tare Weight equals allowable net payload.
02

Exterior Wall Panels & Side Rails

Thorough examination of the corrugated corten-steel side and front wall panels:

  • Indentations & Dents: Measuring inward dents against IICL limits (dents exceeding 50 mm into cargo space reduce volumetric capacity and indicate handling impacts).
  • Holes, Punctures & Tears: Identifying punctures caused by forklift tines, spreader twistlocks, or collisions.
  • Bottom & Top Side Rails: Checking longitudinal structural rails for bowing, kinks, or tears that compromise longitudinal bending resistance.
  • Corrosion Assessment: Differentiating cosmetic surface oxidation from severe flaking scale rust and structural steel thickness loss.
03

Roof Panels & Internal Light-Leakage Test

The roof panel is the most vulnerable surface for water ingress during ocean voyages:

  • Internal Light Test: The surveyor steps inside the container with doors fully locked in daylight; any visible pinholes of daylight immediately reveal roof punctures, weld separations, or gasket breaches.
  • Downward Roof Dents: Dents exceeding 35 mm that collect standing pool water, accelerating rust perforation and corrosion.
  • Roof Bows & Corner Castings Welds: Structural welds connecting roof panels to front header, rear header, and top side rails must be sound and crack-free.
  • Patch Quality: Inspecting previous repair patches to confirm proper full-seam welding and anti-corrosive primer coating.
04

Interior Walls, Bulkheads & Lashing Rings

Checking internal surfaces for cargo protection and securing capabilities:

  • Internal Protrusions: Protruding weld burrs, screws, or splintered repair plates that could tear cargo carton packaging or pallet shrink-wrap.
  • Water Stains: Old water tide marks on wall corrugated corrugations signaling past flooding or chronic leakage.
  • Lashing Points: Verification that all internal lashing rings along bottom and top side rails are intact, securely welded, and capable of holding cargo tie-down straps (rated at 1,000–2,000 kg capacity).
05

Timber Flooring & Understructure Cross-Members

Flooring directly sustains the concentrated wheel loads of forklift trucks during loading (tested up to 5,460 kg axle loads under ISO 1496-1):

  • Floor Delamination & Soft Spots: Checking hardwood/plywood floorboards for water rot, softness, broken veneers, or spongy spots.
  • Protruding Fasteners: Hammering down or documenting exposed screw heads or nails that can puncture bagged goods or pallets.
  • Chemical Stains & Residues: Inspecting dark grease, hydraulic oil, acid, or chemical stains that could damage dry merchandise.
  • Underfloor Cross-Members: Checking steel I-beam floor supports and forklift pockets beneath the container for twists, cracks, or rust detachment.
06

Door Operation, Locking Rods & EPDM Weather Seals

Evaluating container closure mechanisms and weather seals:

  • Smooth Swing Operation: Confirming that left and right door leaves swing open smoothly through a full 270 degrees and latch against side walls without binding.
  • Hinges & Hinge Pins: Checking for seized hinge pins, cracked hinge lugs, or bent bracket mounts.
  • Locking Rods, Cams & Keepers: Ensuring vertical locking bars rotate freely and upper/lower cams lock firmly into keepers without excessive play.
  • Door Gaskets (Rubber Seals): Inspecting continuous double-lip EPDM rubber seals for cuts, hardening, tears, detachment, or foreign matter that permits rainwater infiltration.
  • Customs Seal Hole: Verifying the seal aperture on the locking handle is undamaged and ready for ISO 17712 High Security Bolt Seals.
07

Corner Castings, Posts & Stacking Geometry

Corner castings are the critical lifting interfaces connecting to crane spreaders, reach stackers, and ship twistlocks:

  • Castings Integrity: Inspecting all 8 corner castings (4 top, 4 bottom) for stress cracks, enlarged apertures, or gouges.
  • Corner Post Alignment: Verifying corner posts are plumb and true, without diagonal bowing that causes ship cellular guide jam.
  • Squareness & Dimensions: Checking container diagonal tolerances to ensure lifting frames engage cleanly without twisting stresses.
08

Cleanliness, Hygiene & Odour Elimination

A container must be swept clean, dry, and odor-free before cargo stuffing:

  • Dry Sweeping & Debris: Confirming total removal of previous cargo packaging, timber dunnage, nails, and metal strapping bands.
  • Pest & Infestation Checks: Verifying absence of insects, termites, rodents, bird droppings, or agricultural residues (mandatory for phytosanitary compliance in countries like Australia, USA, and Europe).
  • Odour Evaluation: Checking for strong chemical smells, solvent fumes, fish meal, fertilizer, or smoke odours that could cause cross-tainting of consumer merchandise.

Common Defects Found During Container Inspection

Below is a comparative breakdown of the 10 most common physical defects discovered during empty container surveys, their operational risk, and the standard corrective protocol:

Common Defect Possible Concern & Operational Risk Recommended Remedial Action
Roof Punctures & Holes Direct rainwater entry leading to extensive cargo wetting and water damage. Steel insert patch welding per IICL repair standards; reject container if pervasive.
Flaking Rust & Corrosion Structural steel thinning, weakening panel rigidity and causing eventual breach. Mechanical wire-brushing, zinc primer application, or panel section replacement.
Broken / Soft Flooring Forklift wheel breakthrough during loading, collapsing heavy cargo pallets. Replacement of marine plywood floor section with approved treated timber planks.
Torn Door Gaskets Seepage of windblown dust, humidity, and tropical spray into cargo envelope. Replacement of damaged EPDM door gasket profiles and corner vulcanization.
Bent Locking Bars Impaired door closing; failure to achieve positive seal compression or fit customs bolts. Straightening under hydraulic press or replacement of vertical locking rod assembly.
Cracked Corner Castings Catastrophic lifting failure when hoisted by quayside gantry crane spreaders. Immediate removal from service; certified welding by authorized Lloyd's/IRS facility.
Side Wall Punctures External exposure, moisture ingress, and pilferage vulnerabilities during storage. Corten steel insert patch welding with continuous inner and outer weld seams.
Internal Water Stains Signals intermittent leak points that become active under heavy rain conditions. Rigorous high-pressure water hose testing to identify and seal obscure leak paths.
Seized / Broken Hinges Inability to open doors at destination; risks breaking during discharge operations. Lubrication, hinge pin replacement, or bracket realignment and re-welding.
Persistent Odours / Chemical Stains Cross-contamination of foodstuff, garments, pharmaceuticals, or paper cargo. High-pressure steam washing, chemical neutralization, or floor plank replacement.

Different Types of Shipping Containers Inspected

Inspection procedures are customized according to the container engineering design and intended cargo characteristics:

1. Standard Dry Cargo Containers (20ft & 40ft)

The workhorse of global commerce. Surveys evaluate corrugated side walls, front wall, roof panel, wooden floorboards, door hinges, and locking rods for general freight.

2. High Cube Containers (40ft & 45ft HC)

Providing 9ft 6in external height (one foot higher than standard). Checks verify height warning zebra stripes, gooseneck tunnel clearance, and upper corner post rigidity.

3. Refrigerated (Reefer) Containers

Carrying temperature-sensitive food and pharma. Requires specialized testing of compressor machinery, electrical connections, T-bar flooring airflow, and polyurethane foam wall insulation.

4. Open Top Containers

Used for heavy machinery loaded from overhead cranes. Inspection focuses on removable roof bows, swing-out rear header door mechanisms, and tarpaulin (tilt) condition.

5. Flat Rack & Platform Containers

Designed for out-of-gauge (OOG) industrial cargo and heavy plant equipment. Surveys check heavy-duty steel base frame deflection, folding end-walls, and high-capacity lashing eyes.

6-Step Container Inspection Process

To ensure total repeatability and audit compliance, our surveyors execute the following structured 6-step survey process:

1

Step 1: Container Identification & Verification

Cross-checking container numbers against the booking delivery order (DO), checking ISO dimensions, owner details, and validating CSC plate certification validity.

2

Step 2: Exterior Structural Walk-Around

A 360-degree perimeter walk checking corner posts, top and bottom side rails, side corrugations, front wall, understructure cross-members, and forklift pockets for structural deformation.

3

Step 3: Internal Examination & Light-Leakage Check

The surveyor conducts a darkroom light test inside the container with doors closed to reveal pinholes. Internal walls and roof seams are examined for moisture stains, rust, and wall punctures.

4

Step 4: Floor Integrity & Cleanliness Verification

Tapping and walking across the wooden floor to detect soft rot, checking for chemical staining, protruding fasteners, and confirming absence of pests, moulds, and residual cargo debris.

5

Step 5: Door Mechanism & Gasket Compression Testing

Testing opening and closure ease, checking hinge lubrication, assessing cam/keeper engagement, and checking continuous contact along all 4 edges of the rubber door gasket.

6

Step 6: Photographic Defect Logging & Certified Survey Report

Compiling high-resolution photos into a certified Chartered Engineer Inspection Report detailing findings, defect severity, fitness verdict, and corrective recommendations.

  • Inspection Purpose (Export Staging, On-Hire, Off-Hire)
  • Previous Inspection Reports (if available)
